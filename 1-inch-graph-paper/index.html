Seven columns, and why that is right

An inch is 25.4 mm, so a printable width of 195 mm holds seven whole squares and no more. The bold line is off by default here: at this size the grid is already easy to count, and heavy lines mostly get in the way of what you draw on it.

A4 is the better paper for this size. A4 is taller, and at inch squares that extra height is worth a whole row: 7 by 10 against Letter's 7 by 9 with the bars on. It is the one square size where the metric sheet wins on count.

What inch squares are for

Every use here maps one square to one meaningful unit — a foot, a finished inch, five feet of dungeon corridor — so the count of squares matters less than the fact that each one is whole.

Tabletop battle maps

One inch is the standard grid for miniatures, where a square is five feet of game space. A Letter sheet is a 35 by 45 foot map; tape two together for a larger encounter.

Room layout at 1 inch to the foot

Double the usual architectural scale, for drawing a single room large enough to place furniture on. A Letter sheet covers 7 by 9 feet, so it suits a bathroom or a closet rather than a house.

Quilt layouts

One square per finished inch makes block arithmetic disappear. Draft the layout at size, then add seam allowance when you cut.

Poster and sign layout

Scaling up by hand is easiest when the source grid is coarse. An inch grid transfers cleanly to a wall or board grid at any multiple.

Large early number work

For counting, sorting and one-object-per-square activities where anything smaller is fiddly for small hands.

Garden bed planning

One square to one foot lets you plan spacing by counting squares, which is how most planting guides express it anyway.

Before you print

A five percent shrink turns an inch square into about 24 mm, which looks entirely normal and silently breaks every scale on the page — a mini on a 24 mm grid no longer sits where the rules say it does. Set the scale to 100% or Actual Size and check the 4 in bar: it should span exactly 4 squares. The full setting-by-setting guide is on the home page.

Questions about inch grids

Why does a Letter sheet only fit 7 columns?

Because 8.5 inches minus the printing margins leaves about 7.7 inches of printable width, and only seven whole inch squares fit inside that. The remainder is split between the two margins so the grid sits centred rather than crowding one edge.

Is 1 inch the same as 2.5 cm?

No, an inch is 25.4 mm. The 1.6 percent difference is invisible on one square and reaches nearly 3 mm across seven, which is enough to misalign a printed sheet against anything measured in inches.

Can I use this for D&D or other tabletop games?

Yes, one inch is the standard grid for miniature play, with one square representing five feet. A Letter sheet at 7 by 9 squares is a 35 by 45 foot area. Print several and butt them edge to edge, or switch to Tabloid for 10 by 15 squares on one sheet.

Should I turn the heavy lines on?

They are off by default because an inch grid is already easy to count. Setting the field to 5 gives five-square blocks, which helps on a large map where you are counting distances repeatedly.

How do I get more squares on one page?

Switch to A4 for an extra row, turn the measuring bars off for another, or move to Legal or Tabloid. Tabloid holds 10 by 15 inch squares, which is the largest single-sheet grid available here.

Is an inch grid useful for quilting?

For layout, yes. One square is one finished inch, so a block plan can be counted rather than calculated. Seam allowance is added at cutting, not on the plan, so the drawing stays in finished dimensions throughout.
