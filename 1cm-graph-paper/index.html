What a centimetre grid gives you

Ten squares make 10 centimetres and the bold line defaults to every tenth square, so each block is a 10 cm reference square. Fewer, larger squares is the whole point here: a page holds under five hundred of them, and every one is big enough to hold a written digit.

Why 19 columns on A4 and not 21? A 210 mm sheet holds 21 centimetres edge to edge, but printers cannot reach the edge. Take a 10 mm margin off each side and 19 whole squares remain, centred, with the leftover split between the two margins.

Where a centimetre grid is the right choice

Reach for this size when legibility matters more than how much fits on the page.

Area and perimeter

Each square is one square centimetre, so a shape drawn on the grid can be measured by counting rather than calculating. This is the reason it is standard in primary maths.

Coordinate work with labels

A centimetre square holds a two digit number comfortably. For teaching a coordinate plane, that means the axis values can be written on the grid itself rather than squeezed alongside it.

Plans at 1:100

One square to one metre is the metric equivalent of quarter inch to the foot. An A4 sheet at 19 by 25 squares covers 19 by 25 metres, enough for a flat or a small garden on one page.

Early number work

One digit per box keeps columns aligned in long addition and subtraction, which is a common reason a young student gets the method right and the answer wrong.

Large print and low vision

Paired with black or grey lines and the measuring bars switched off, a centimetre grid is far easier to work on than the 5 mm default.

Planting and layout sketches

Coarse enough to sketch on freehand while still being a real measurement, which suits garden beds, furniture layouts and seating plans.

Before you print

A page shrunk by the usual four to six percent turns a centimetre square into about 9.5 mm, which is close enough to three eighths of an inch to pass unnoticed and wrong enough to break a 1:100 plan. Set the scale to 100% or Actual Size and check the 100 mm bar with a ruler — on this grid it should span exactly ten squares. The full setting-by-setting guide is on the home page.

Questions about centimetre grids

Is 1 cm graph paper the same as 1/2 inch?

No. Half an inch is 12.7 mm, 27 percent wider than a centimetre, and a half inch square has about 61 percent more area. They are the natural large-square choice in their own measurement systems, which is why they get substituted for each other, but they are not interchangeable on a scale drawing.

How many centimetre squares fit on a page?

On A4 with the measuring bars on, 19 across by 25 down. Without the bars it becomes 19 by 27. Letter gives 19 by 24 with the bars and 19 by 25 without, since Letter is a little wider and noticeably shorter than A4.

Can I use it for a 1:100 floor plan?

Yes, and it is the cleanest way to draw one by hand. At 1:100 a centimetre on paper is a metre in reality, so one square is one metre and a room is counted rather than measured. An A4 sheet covers 19 by 25 metres. The plan is only to scale if the page prints at 100 percent.

Should the heavy lines be every 10 squares or every 5?

Every 10 is the default here and gives 10 cm reference blocks. Every 5 gives 5 cm blocks and is easier to count against on a busy drawing. Set the field to 0 if the bold lines are getting in the way of the work.

Is a centimetre grid too big for secondary school maths?

For plotting a dense function it is, and 5 mm is the usual choice there. A centimetre grid earns its place where each square has to carry something written inside it, or where the drawing will be read at a distance.

Can I get centimetre squares on Letter paper?

Yes, change the paper selector and the squares stay exactly 10 mm; only the count changes. Choose the paper that is actually loaded, because printing A4 artwork onto Letter forces a resize the scale setting cannot prevent.
